Bloom Studio Boston:
Designing & Building a Digital Home

A responsive booking website for a real Boston creative workshop business — designed from information architecture through visual system, then built as a working full-stack product.

Role UX/UI Designer + Engineer
Platform Responsive Web
Tools Figma, React, Node.js
Scope UX + Full-Stack Build

Bloom Studio is a real Boston workshop business. This site was designed and built as freelance client work, from UX strategy through full-stack implementation.

Workshop discovery (desktop) · Booking flow · Private events · FAQ (mobile)

01

Context

Bloom Studio Boston is a creative workshop business offering hands-on experiences like candle making, decorative clock making, mask design, and seasonal crafts. It already had a visual identity, workshop photography, and a social media presence, but no dedicated website that brought the full experience together.

Customers could discover Bloom through Instagram or a third-party booking page, but there was no central place to understand the brand, browse workshops, compare upcoming dates, learn about private events, or build a longer-term relationship with the studio. I saw that missing digital home as the project: design it, then build it.

My role

UX/UI Designer and UX Engineer. I owned information architecture, interaction design, visual design, and responsive design, then implemented the product in React and Node.js/Express rather than stopping at high-fidelity screens.

02

The Challenge

Bloom already had the ingredients of a strong local brand — distinctive workshops, inviting imagery, a feminine visual identity, and an experience built around creativity and community. What it lacked was a digital home. The existing journey depended on stitching together separate platforms: discover on Instagram, follow a link to an event, then book somewhere else entirely.

That fragmentation left basic questions unanswered — what's available, when, how much, what's included, can I bring a group, can I host a private event, where's the studio, and if I loved it, how do I come back? Rather than designing another event marketplace, I wanted the site to feel like walking into Bloom itself: warm, personal, and approachable.

The goal

Create a responsive digital home where a customer can discover a workshop, understand the experience, find a date, and start booking — without losing the personality of the physical studio. The core journey: Discover → Explore → Choose a workshop → Select a date → Choose guests → Cart → Checkout.

03

Key Design Decisions

Information architecture, not a marketplace

I organized the site around the business itself rather than isolated ticket listings: Home → Workshops → Workshop Details → Booking → Cart, with About, Private Events, FAQ, Account, and Membership as supporting destinations. Early concepts leaned on marketplace patterns — more filters, more booking controls — but Bloom isn't a marketplace with hundreds of listings, it's a curated local studio. I simplified discovery to a small set of categories and let large photography and clear calls to action carry the page instead.

Visual direction

The visual language extends Bloom's existing brand: a warm cream background, muted terracotta accents, soft blush and blue details, editorial photography, rounded cards, and an expressive serif paired with a simple sans-serif interface font. It had to feel feminine and artistic without turning childish, and polished enough to support paid experiences without reading as a generic lifestyle template.

Responsive by design, not by shrinking

Discovery mostly happens through links shared on social media, so the interface direction started mobile-first. But I didn't want the finished product to just be a mobile screen stretched into a desktop browser. On mobile, workshop cards stack vertically and booking actions stay within reach. On tablet, cards move into multiple columns. On desktop, the experience becomes a full site — multi-column grids, split layouts, larger photography, side-by-side booking information — all from the same underlying product.

A real workshop calendar

Availability doesn't rely on hard-coded strings like "Saturday, November 16." The system stores each workshop's scheduling rules — weekday, start and end time, capacity, booking window, lead time — and the server generates upcoming sessions relative to the actual current date. The Workshops page has a full calendar for date-based browsing, made collapsible so it isn't forced on every visitor, and the booking page shows only the dates relevant to that workshop.

Booking and cart

An early version of date selection scrolled horizontally through every available slot — technically functional, but awkward on large screens. I replaced it with a responsive date grid that reduces gracefully on smaller devices. Guest count and total are calculated dynamically: a $68 workshop only becomes $136 once the customer actually selects two guests. The cart itself is functional, not just styled — quantities, removals, and subtotals are calculated on the backend from the current workshop price, so nothing depends on a value trusted from the browser.

Private events and studio information

Private events are a primary offering, not a small contact link. Customers select an event type — birthday, bachelorette, corporate, custom celebration — which updates the inquiry form and moves them into a real request flow that posts to a backend endpoint. Studio location and hours are stored centrally so the footer, location section, and directions all pull from one source instead of being repeated by hand.

04

Expanding the Product Beyond a One-Time Booking

As I worked through the booking experience, I started thinking about what happens after a customer attends their first workshop. Bloom is the kind of business where repeat visits and community matter, so I identified an opportunity to design for returning customers rather than treating every visitor like a first-time guest. These were product opportunities I introduced during the design process, not existing Bloom Studio offerings — concepts for future validation, not a claim that the business should launch them immediately.

Customer profiles

Accounts so returning guests can eventually access booking history, saved information, membership benefits, and a faster repeat-booking experience. Passwords are hashed for the prototype; a production deployment would move authentication to production-grade infrastructure.

Bloom Club membership

A proposed monthly membership concept — one workshop credit per month, a discount on additional classes, early access to new dates, and member-only events. Presented as a concept for the business to validate, not something already live.

05

Engineering the Experience

Rather than stopping at high-fidelity screens, I translated the design into a functioning full-stack prototype. The front end uses reusable React components for workshop cards, calendars, navigation, forms, and account states, so workshop information isn't duplicated across pages. On the backend, Express manages workshop data, generated sessions, carts, private-event inquiries, newsletter subscriptions, customer accounts, and membership-interest submissions.

Building it — not just designing it — surfaced assumptions that stay invisible in static screens: empty carts, descriptions of different lengths, months with different numbers of days, one workshop with eight future dates and another with two, forms that can succeed or fail.

  • Standardized card heightSo primary actions align consistently even when descriptions run different lengths.
  • Replaced horizontal scroll with a responsive gridThe original date-selection row worked technically but forced awkward sideways scanning on large screens.
  • Moved the full calendar off the Home pageIt demanded too much attention too early in the journey; Home now surfaces a few upcoming sessions instead.
  • Made the Workshops calendar collapsibleSo date-based browsing is available without being forced on every visitor.
  • Separated membership interest from real billingRecurring billing is a business decision requiring payment infrastructure — collecting interest doesn't presume that decision has been made.

Outcome

✓
A functional, responsive prototype

Workshop discovery, dynamically generated availability, guest selection, and a server-backed cart all work end to end, not just as static screens.

✓
A digital home that matches the brand

The visual system carries Bloom's warmth and identity across discovery, booking, private events, and studio information rather than defaulting to a generic marketplace feel.

✓

→
Next: validate with real customers

This hasn't been measured through a live launch, so the next step is usability testing the booking flow and measuring workshop-detail-to-booking conversion, calendar engagement, cart completion, and private-event inquiry completion.

07

What I Learned

01 Design and engineering are one conversation

Designing answered what the experience should feel like. Building it exposed how that experience needed to actually behave — especially around dates, cart logic, and state.

02 Static screens hide real-world edge cases

Empty carts, uneven description lengths, and months with different day counts only became visible once the prototype had real state to handle.

03 Not every feature belongs at the same point in the journey

A full calendar helps during booking but distracts during discovery. An account benefits a repeat customer without needing to interrupt a first-time visitor.

04 Restraint is a design skill

Proposing customer profiles and Bloom Club as concepts for validation — rather than presenting them as shipped features — was as much a design decision as any screen.

The project became less about building "a website" and more about designing a digital service around an existing physical experience — then proving it could actually run.
