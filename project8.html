Research Artifacts

01

Context

CURATE started as a founder's concept for a fashion discovery platform — a place for people to find current and emerging trends. A high-fidelity design already existed by the time I joined, built around an ambitious nav: Fashion, Beauty, Culture, Lifestyle, Music, News, Shopping, Curated. It looked polished. It also hadn't been tested against a single real user.

Rather than treat the existing design as a finished product, I proposed we treat it as a concept to evaluate — not evidence of user need, and not a locked information architecture. That distinction shaped every decision that followed.

The starting stance

An interface can look confident and still be standing on an untested assumption. My job was to find out whether the breadth of that nav reflected real user need or just early scope creep, before designing another pixel around it.

02

The Pre-Research Brief

Before any research began, I wrote a discovery brief that named what was actually known versus assumed — and it wasn't much. The founder had described the product in a sentence. Everything else — audience, how a "trend" gets defined, where content comes from, whether shopping was core to the business model — was open.

I framed six questions as blocking, meaning I didn't think further design work should proceed confidently until they had answers:

Scope & audience
  • Is this a fashion trend-discovery product, or a broader fashion/culture/lifestyle destination — as the existing nav implied?
  • Who does the company actually envision as the primary user?
Definition & content
  • How does the company define a "trend," and how would the platform determine what's currently trending?
  • Where does content come from — company, users, creators, brands, or a mix?
Design flexibility
  • Is the existing high-fidelity design a fixed requirement, or is the UX team free to reconsider it based on research?
Business model
  • Is there an intended revenue model, and is shopping meant to be core to the experience or a supporting action?

I also mapped a longer list of open questions across product purpose, content sourcing, IA scope, the role of creators and brands, and business goals — and laid out a research plan in five phases: stakeholder clarification, secondary research on fashion discovery behavior, competitive and analogous research, primary user research, and synthesis that would classify every early assumption as Supported, Partially Supported, Not Supported, or Still Unknown.

03

Primary Research

I ran a 21-person exploratory survey on how people discover, evaluate, and keep fashion inspiration — structured and short-answer questions, no follow-up interviews. I was explicit about that boundary throughout: with n = 21 and no interview layer, every finding is directional, not a validated conclusion, and I said so on every synthesis document rather than only once at the start.

71%discover trends via TikTok — the top channel by far
15/21save or bookmark a style before doing anything else
71%say friends they know are their most trusted source
8/21rate their fashion interest a 5 out of 5 — a mixed-involvement sample

The open-ended answers carried as much signal as the multiple-choice ones. On why generic recommendations lose people: "It can be hard to discover your own personal style and taste when you're being forced to fit into a specific one." On price and fit: "A lot of fashion inspiration on social media tends to be out of my price range or made of poor material." On the traceability problem: "When a person is showing their outfit and they do not put links or they don't say where they bought it."

I sorted the full set of responses — structured patterns and open-ended wording alike — into seven affinity themes: discovery is social and often passive, personal relevance matters, price/fit/representation shape relevance, saving is a recurring part of the journey, discovery often leads to more research, trust and trend recognition are socially reinforced, and repetition/ads/poor item traceability create friction. Each theme card kept participant wording visually distinct from my own synthesis, specifically so a theme summary never got mistaken for a direct quote or a product requirement.

The friction and trust themes

Two themes didn't make it into most of my early screens but shaped them anyway. On friction: 11 respondents cited a personal-style mismatch as the top reason content felt irrelevant, ahead of price (7) and repetitive content (7). On trust: 15/21 pointed to repeated social-media exposure and 15/21 to friends/people they know as their most trusted signal — one institutional "trending" badge was consistently less persuasive than seeing something repeat across people.

04

Synthesis: From Evidence to Needs

To avoid turning survey patterns directly into features, I ran every theme through the same four-part structure: what the evidence showed, what it might mean, the user need it implied, and the design question it left open. That discipline is what kept "people save things a lot" from silently becoming "build Pinterest-style boards" without ever being tested.

  • Discovery — Evidence: 15/21 discover via TikTok, 9/21 mostly while scrolling. Need: people need to encounter relevant inspiration even without a specific search term. JTBD: "When I want fashion inspiration but don't know exactly what I'm looking for, I want to browse relevant looks so I can discover ideas that interest me."
  • Relevance — Evidence: 14/21 stop for visual appeal, 12/21 for personal-style fit, 10/21 for body/size fit, 10/21 for affordability. Need: people need to quickly judge whether inspiration fits their style, body, and budget. JTBD: "When I see a look that interests me, I want to understand whether it fits my style, body, and budget so I can decide whether it's useful to me."
  • Saving — Evidence: 15/21 save/bookmark after finding something they like; Pinterest boards (12) and TikTok favorites (9) are the common tools. Need: people need to preserve inspiration and return to it later. JTBD: "When I find a look I may want later, I want to save it in a way I can find again so I can return when I'm ready to style, research, or shop."
  • Next step — Evidence: 12/21 want styling guidance, 11/21 want more photos/examples. Need: people need practical context to understand and apply what they find. JTBD: "When I discover a trend or look I like, I want to see clear examples and styling context so I can understand how I might wear it myself."
  • Item traceability — Evidence: 7/21 search for the actual clothing after finding a style; 5 respondents reported difficulty finding it. Need: people need a clear path from inspiration to item information when they choose to investigate further. JTBD: "When I want to act on a look I found, I want to identify the item or find similar options so I don't have to restart the search elsewhere."
  • Trend credibility — Evidence: 15/21 read repeated exposure as a trend signal, 15/21 trust friends over institutions. Need: people need enough context to judge whether a "trend" label is credible. JTBD: "When something is labeled as a trend, I want enough context to judge whether that label feels credible before I pay attention to it."

I explicitly flagged what the data did not prove: that the product needs a TikTok-style algorithm, Pinterest-style boards specifically, on-platform checkout, or that any of it generalizes past a young, comfort-first sample (20 of 21 respondents were 18–34; 12 of 21 described themselves as comfort/function-first over trend-chasing).

05

Persona

From the synthesis I built a single directional proto-persona — Maya Thompson, 23, Boston, a recent grad who's interested in fashion without being a hardcore trend-chaser. I labeled her explicitly as a starting point for MVP prioritization, to be refined with future research, not a finished user model.

"I want outfit inspiration that actually feels like me — and that I can afford, wear, and find again later."

Her key behaviors, goals, and pain points map directly back to the survey: discovers mostly by scrolling TikTok and Pinterest, saves through bookmarks and screenshots, researches styling and similar items after finding a look, and gets frustrated by content that's repetitive, unaffordable, or doesn't reflect her body or fit needs.

06

From Insight to System

Before designing a single screen, I mapped a conceptual system diagram: five core product principles — passive + active discovery, personal relevance, save + revisit, styling context, and shopping as a supporting action rather than the point — set against inputs (user behavior signals, trend content, product information, optional creator input), the platform core (discover feed, search + filters, trend detail, save/collections, a relevance layer, content taxonomy, trend credibility signals), and the resulting user value (relevant inspiration, better trend understanding, saved looks, an easier path to similar items, repeat engagement).

That diagram also carried the open questions I still didn't have enough evidence to close: how broad the IA should be beyond fashion, how to split navigation versus filters, how trends should be labeled and explained, and how much personalization an MVP should actually attempt. I mapped the core MVP user flow as eight steps — open Discover, browse or search, see a look, evaluate relevance against style/body/budget, open trend detail, view styling examples and item info, save or keep exploring, optionally visit similar items or retailer links — directly reflecting the discovery loop the survey data traced: scroll, notice, save, look up, return.

07

Design Execution

Only after the system diagram and open questions were documented did I move into screens — low-fidelity desktop wireframes first, across Discover, Search & Explore, Trend Detail, and Saved, to pressure-test structure and information hierarchy before any visual design decisions. Once the structure held up, I used Stitch, an AI-assisted design tool, to move quickly from validated wireframes into high-fidelity starting points, then refined those into the final UI and extended the same flows to mobile.

🧭 Discover feed

A personalized stream of trends and looks built for passive browsing, for the majority who discover while scrolling rather than searching.

🎯 Preference onboarding

Style, aesthetic, and budget inputs collected upfront — directly addressing personal-style mismatch, the single most-cited relevance problem in the survey.

📄 Trend detail pages

"Why it's trending" and "how people are styling it" sections, plus similar-item and related-inspiration modules, answering both the styling-context need and the social-trust finding.

🔖 Search & saved

Faceted search by style, category, occasion, price, source, and fit, plus a dedicated saved/collections space for the single most common behavior in the entire survey.

Iteration rounds

The screens went through three distinct rounds rather than one pass:

  • Low-fi — information architecture Desktop wireframes across Discover, Search & Explore, Trend Detail, and Saved, focused entirely on structure and hierarchy before any visual identity was applied.
  • A/B — high-fidelity screens against business goals Two high-fidelity directions built to satisfy the founder's stated product goals — personalization-forward discovery, clear preference capture, and a straightforward path from inspiration to similar items — and evaluated against each other before moving forward.
  • C/D — revision screens toward an editorial direction A further revision pass once the future direction shifted toward a more editorial, Vogue-style UI — cleaner typographic hierarchy, more whitespace, imagery treated as content rather than decoration — so the design system could grow into that direction rather than needing a rebuild.

I tracked design decisions and open style questions in a running Google Sheets tracker alongside the Figma file, and separately explored a wordmark direction for the CURATE brand — testing several serif treatments before settling on a direction that felt editorial rather than app-generic.

Outcomes

Every core screen — discover feed, preference onboarding, trend detail, search, saved collections — maps back to a cited survey finding rather than a guess about what a fashion app "should" have.

✓

09

What I Learned

01 An existing design isn't evidence

Walking in with a high-fidelity design already built made it tempting to skip straight to polish. Treating it as an unvalidated concept instead — not a requirement — was the decision that shaped everything after it.

02 Directional research still earns its keep

A 21-person survey without interviews can't carry a product roadmap alone. It can still expose real tension points — passive discovery, relevance over popularity, saving as a first-class behavior — worth designing around, as long as I stayed honest about the boundary.

03 Separating evidence from interpretation is a discipline, not a formality

It's easy to let "people save things" become "build Pinterest boards." Holding those apart, on paper, produced better open questions than jumping straight to a feature.

04 Naming what you don't know is part of the deliverable

The open UX questions I couldn't yet answer were as useful to hand off as the screens I could ship — they told whoever picked this up next exactly where the real risk still lived.

Good screens are easy to admire and easy to build on a bad foundation. The work that mattered here happened before any screen existed — asking what people actually needed, and being disciplined enough not to answer that question with a guess.
